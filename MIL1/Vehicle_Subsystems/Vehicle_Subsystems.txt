on vehicle safety. A vehicle braking system is responsible for slowing down or stopping
the vehicle when needed. There are two primary types of brakes, drum brakes and disc brakes.
A drum brake consists of a metal rotating drum and friction coated surfaces called brake shoes.
When the driver applies the brake, these shoes press against the drum, creating friction that
slows the vehicle down. Drum brakes are more common in older vehicles and are still used
on the rear wheels of some modern cars due to their cost effectiveness. Disc brakes, on
the other hand, are positioned inside the wheel and rely on an hydraulic system to function.
When the brake pedal is pressed, hydraulic pressure forces the brake pads to clamp onto a rotating
metal disc or rotor, slowing the wheel. Disc brakes offer superior stopping power and are widely
used in modern vehicles.
Now let's talk about vehicle dynamics, specifically overseer and underseer. Overseers occurs when
a car's rear wheels lose traction during a turn, causing the vehicle to turn more sharply than intended.
This behavior can lead to skidding or spinning, particularly in rear-wheel drive cars, or in conditions
of low traction. Underseer, in contrast, happens when the front wheels lose traction, causing the
car to follow a wider path than intended. Drivers often experience understeer in front wheel-driver
cars when turning at high speeds or during sudden maneuvers. Correcting understeer typically involves
reducing speed and realigning the steering.
Moving on to the anti-lock brake system, or ABS. ABS prevents wheels lockup during braking by
continuously monitoring the rotational speed of each wheel. Sensors placed on each wheel relay
this information to the electronic control unit . If the ECU detects that a wheel is about
to lockup, it modulates the brake pressure to that wheel, allowing it to maintain traction.
This system enhances vehicle stability and reduces stopping distances, particularly on slippery surfaces.
Electronic brake force distribution, or EBD, works in conjunction with ABS to optimize braking performance.
During braking, the low distribution on the wheels varies, with the rear wheels generally experiencing
less grip. EBD adjusts the braking force applied to each wheel to prevent rear wheel lockup and
maintain vehicle stability, especially during cornering. Electronic stability control, also known
as ESP or DSC, is a system designed to enhance vehicle stability during skidding events. The ECU monitors the
car's trajectory and compares it to the driver's intended path. If a discrepancy is detected, the system reduces engine power
and applies braking to individual wheels to correct the car's motion. This intervention prevents accidents
by helping the driver to maintain control.
The traction control system, also known as anti-spin regulation, prevents wheel spin during acceleration.
This system is particularly useful in low traction conditions such as rain or ice. Sensors detect wheel spin,
and DSU adjusts engine power or applies braking to spinning wheel, ensuring optimal traction.
The heel holder system assists drivers when starting on an incline. It prevents the vehicle from rolling
backward during the transition from the brake pedal to the accelerator, offering convenience and reducing
the risk of accidents. Alternators are responsible for keeping the vehicle's battery charged and powering
electrical components. Since alternating current EC cannot be stored, alternators use a rectifier to convert
DC to direct current DC for storage. Modern vehicles often feature smart alternators, say SAM,
which adjusts the power drawn from the engine based on the vehicle's operational state, improving efficiency.
The starter motor is a key component in an internal combustion engine.
It's primary function is to draw electrical current from the battery to turn the engine over until it reaches
a speed sufficient for self-sustaining operation. It is mechanically connected to the engine's
craft shafts and engaged only during the startup phase. Modern starter motors are compact yet powerful,
and ensuring reliable ignition in all conditions. Next, we will discuss emission-related subsystems,
crucial for reducing the environmental impact of vehicles.
Exhaust gas recirculation system recirculates a little portion of exhaust
exhaust gases back into the engine's intake main fold. By doing so, it reduces combustion temperatures
and the formation of nitrogen oxides, or NOx, a harmful pollutant. The AGR system is controlled by the
engine control unit, which adjusts the recirculation rate using a PWM signal.
The diesel particulate filter, or DPF, is designed to trap and remove particulate matter from diesel exhaust.
It works in conjunction with the catalytic converter and uses diagnostic software to monitor and maintain
its functionality. Periodic regeneration burns off accumulated salt, ensuring efficient operation.
The lambda probe is a sensor that measures the oxygen concentration in a vehicle's exhaust gases.
By comparing this measurement to the oxygen levels in the ambient air,
it determines the air-fuel mixture in the engine. The lambda value indicates whether the mixture
is stichiometric, where lambda is equal to 1, rich, if lambda is minus than 1, or lean, if lambda is
greater than 1. This information helps the engine control unit in optimizing fuel injection and in reducing emissions.
There are different types of lambda probes, zirconium dioxide, titanium dioxide, and double lambda probes.
Zirconium dioxide probes reproduce a voltage signal based on oxygen levels, while titanium
dioxide probes measure resistance changes. Double lambda probes enhance precision by using two sensors
to provide more accurate data, improving engine efficiency and reducing emissions.
A catalytic converter reduces harmful emissions by converting pollutants into less harmful substances.
It operates within the exhaust system, using a catalytic to promote chemical reactions that oxidize
carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons while using nitrogen oxides. The catalytic converter also contributes
to noise reduction in the exhaust system. There are three main types of catalytic converters,
reducing, oxidizing, and three-way. Reducing converters target nitrogen oxides, oxidizing converters
handle carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons, and three-way converters combine this function for engines
operating with the stoichiometric combustion. These devices are essential for meeting modern emission standard.
Selective catalytic reduction is a process used to reduce nitrogen oxides in diesel engine exhaust.
By introducing a reducing agent, typically urea, into the exhaust system, the system promotes
chemical reactions that convert NOx into harmless nitrogen and water vapor. The SCR system requires precise dosing
and a specially designed catalyst to ensure effectiveness.
Let's now pass to valve timing, which is essential for efficient operation of a four-stroke engine.
Valve timing refers to the synchronization of the opening and closing of the engine's intake
and exhaust valves with the movement of the pistons.
This synchronization is achieved using chains, belts, or gears to link the camshaft and crankshaft.
Accurate valve timing ensures optimal engine performance, fuel efficiency, and reduced emissions.
Fixed valve timing uses a camshaft with a constant profile, resulting in unchanging valve operation
of the system. This simplicity is cost-effective, but limits optimization across varying engine speeds.
Variable valve timing, on the other hand, dynamically adjusts the timing and duration of valve operation.
This system improves performance at different engine speeds, enhances fuel efficiency, and reduces emissions.
For example, at low RPM, valve timing may prioritize smoothness, while at high RPM, it shifts to maximize power output.
There are several mechanisms to control valve timing.
Camshaft rotation, that adjusts the timing without altering the duration.
Double-toothed gear, that incorporates a gear that can slide, changing the valve timing.
While variation of cam effects alters the cam's impact on valve operation,
influencing the duration and amplitude of timing.
These methods allow manufacturers to fine-tune engine performance based on specific requirements.
Some engines employ advanced configuration, such as double cam, where multiple cams with different
profiles control the same valve, offering flexibility for different driving conditions.
Rocker arm, that uses a single cam and a rocker arm system to adjust the valve's actuation,
and this allows fine control over the valve's behavior.
Or disengageable cams, where these cams can slide along the shaft and lock bolts in a closed position, enabling the engine to deactivate certain cylinders, improving fuel efficiency during load-demand scenarios.
Automotive manufacturers have developed various variable timing systems.
Examples include VTAC by Honda, that optimizes performance and efficiency through multi-stage cam profiles.
VTAC by Honda, that offers seamless adjustment to camshaft timing.
Or multi-air by Fiat, that uses an electronically controlled hydraulic system for precise valve timing and lift control.
This system combines flexibility and efficiency, reducing emissions while enhancing power.
Let's open a parenthesis on multi-air system.
Fiat's multi-air system represents a leap in valve timing technology.
It uses an hydraulic tupper controlled by an electronic valve to modify the camshaft's influence on the valves.
This innovation enables precise control of valve opening and closing, adapting to driving condition and throttle input.
The result is improved power delivery, reduced emissions, and better fuel economy.
Now let's move on the transmission system, which plays a key role in transferring engine power to the wheels while optimizing torque and speed.
Transmissions act as speed reducers, allowing the engine to operate efficiently across varying demands, whether for acceleration, steady cruising, or climbing.
The basis of transmission system is the gearbox.
It modifies the torque coming from the engine by selecting different gear ratios.
Lower gears provide higher torque, useful for climbing or acceleration, while higher gears focus on speed and fuel efficiency during cruising.
Modern gearboxes are designed to minimize power losses and maximize efficiency, offering smooth transitions between gears for optimal driving experiences.
Transmissions can be manual, automatic, or a combination of both.
Automated manual transmission, or AMTS, combines the mechanics of manual gearbox with automated clutch and gear shifted controls.
An electronic control unit manages these operations, providing the efficiency of a manual system with the convenience of automation.
Dual clutch transmissions combine the benefits of manual and automatic systems.
They use two separate clutches for odd and even gears, enabling rapid gear changes with minimal power interruption.
This design enhances both performance and fuel efficiency, making it a preferred choice in high-performance and luxury vehicles.
Automatic transmissions use hydraulic systems and planetary gear sets to change gears seamlessly.
The torque converter replaces the clutch, enabling smooth power transfer and eliminating the need for manual gear changes.
This system prioritizes comfort and easy to use.
To the torque converter is a critical component of automatic transmission.
Positioned between the engine and the transmission, it replaces the traditional clutch.
It enables smooth power transfer during acceleration and allows the engine to idle without stalling the vehicle.
Additionally, it provides torque multiplication at low speeds, improving vehicle performance.
Finally, let's discuss the traction system, which determines how power is distributed to the wheels.
The choice of traction system significantly impacts a vehicle's handling and performance in different driving conditions.
Front wheel drive or FWD.
Here the engine powers the front wheels, offering better fuel efficiency and compact design.
For the rear wheel drive or FWD, the power is sent to the rear wheels, enhancing handling and performance, especially in sports car.
But we have also the four wheel drive or FWD, where power is equally distributed to all wheels, making it ideal for off-road and rugged terrain.
Finally, the all wheel drive or AWD, where it dynamically distributes power between axles based on traction needs, ensuring optimal performance in varying road conditions.
